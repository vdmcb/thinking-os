# Human voice

Packets must read as if a careful colleague wrote them. AI-patterned prose adds cognitive load even when the content is right: the reader spends attention on decoding style instead of understanding claims. These rules are informed by Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" and the blader/humanizer pattern set, adapted to analytical packets.

Apply them while writing and again during the compression pass. They never justify dropping a caveat, number, or conflict.

## Sentences

- One idea per sentence. If a sentence needs three commas, split it.
- Use plain verbs. Write "is", "has", "shows", not "serves as", "boasts", "features", "showcases".
- Active voice with a named actor: "the model applies the pilot rate to all regions", not "the rate is applied".
- No forced groups of three. Two items or four are fine; triads only when the source has three things.
- No "not X but Y" scaffolding. State Y.
- No dramatic fragments or punchline rows. One short sentence lands harder when it is rare.

## Words

Do not use: delve, pivotal, crucial, testament, tapestry, showcase, boasts, landscape (figurative), foster, vibrant, breathtaking, nestled, renowned, seamless, robust (figurative), holistic, leverage (as a verb), utilize, underscore, elevate.

Do not use filler: "in order to" (write "to"), "due to the fact that" (write "because"), "at this point in time" (write "now"), "it is worth noting that" (just note it), "it is important to note" (same).

## Patterns from live review

- No dramatic reveals. Banned: "the catch", "the one thing that changes", "the whole game", "here's the thing", and any sentence built to stage a revelation with a colon. State the fact at the same volume as its neighbors.
- No document personification. A deck does not count, believe, assume, want, or hope. Write what it presents, shows, states, or fails to show.
- No inherited shorthand. The source's internal vocabulary ("the book", "the sandbox", a product codename) gets one plain introduction before any use, or gets replaced with a description. The reader may never open the source.
- No confirmations of the expected. Passed checks, met baselines, and things working as they should earn no words.

## Structure

- No preamble that announces content ("Let's look at", "Here's what you need to know"). State the fact.
- No fake-depth openers ("At its core", "The real question is", "What really matters").
- No heading restated as the first sentence under it.
- No generic endings ("The future looks bright", "Only time will tell"). End on the last concrete fact.
- No defending against objections nobody raised, and no dismissing alternatives nobody proposed.
- Sentence case in headings, not Title Case.

## Typography

- Never em-dashes or en-dashes, including in ranges: write "3-5", not "3–5". Use a comma, colon, period, or parentheses where a dash would go.
- No emoji and no decorative symbols. The claim map's functional marks (the check mark and the question mark) are the only exception.
- Bold and italics only where they carry information the reader needs to find again, as in the question format's field labels. Never bold for emphasis alone.
- Straight quotes.

## Tone

- No chatbot residue: "I hope this helps", "Let me know if", "Great question", "Certainly".
- No hedging stacks: "might" or "could", never "could potentially possibly".
- No praise of the source and no scorn for it. The packet reports; the reviewer judges.

## Questions speak the reviewer's language

The questions are read twice: once by the reviewer deciding whether to send them, once by the author answering them. Both must understand the question on first read. Analyst vocabulary fails both readers.

Write the intent first, as the reviewer would say it out loud. The confusion itself is the question. "The deck counts in agents on one page and clients on another, and I cannot tell if 20 agents means 20 clients" is a better question than any request for a "counting-unit dictionary".

The skill's contract names classes of answer artifacts: counting-unit dictionary, population bridge, evidence-to-claim bridge, authorization schedule, accountability and exception matrix, per-input provenance. These are internal vocabulary. In a question, describe the object in the author's own terms instead:

- not "a counting-unit dictionary", but "a line that defines what you count as one agent, one client, and one live agent, and how the 20, the 1.2%, and the 5-per-quarter map onto each other"
- not "per-input provenance", but "for each number, whether it is from a signed contract, something you measured, or an assumption"
- not "fully loaded cost breakdown", but "what the CHF 456k covers: just salaries, or also taxes, benefits, tools, and office"
- not "a reconciliation", but "the spreadsheet that gets from A to B"

Terms that need a finance or research background are banned from questions: fully loaded, provenance, cross-tabulation, unit economics (as jargon), denominators, cohort, basis point, materiality. If the reviewer would have to ask what the question means, the question has failed before it was sent.

Test each question with the send test: could the reviewer read it once, aloud, and explain to a colleague what is being asked and why it matters, without having read the source? If not, rewrite.

## The read-aloud test

Before returning, read one paragraph aloud (or simulate it). If a sentence would make a colleague pause, rewrite it. If a phrase could appear unchanged in any packet about any document, it is carrying no information; cut it.
